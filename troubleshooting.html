Cloud troubleshooting

Production faults that cross the console, the host, the container, and the pipeline. The aim is a restored service and a reason it should not fail the same way again.

Where the fault usually sits

  • AWS and Azure configuration that drifted from what people believe is deployed
  • Linux hosts, including estates large enough that one snowflake server matters
  • Docker, ECS microservices, and Kubernetes on EKS, AKS, or on-premises clusters
  • Azure App Services, WebJobs, Functions, and the way they are released
  • CI/CD that promoted something it should have stopped

What you leave with

  • The cause, separated from the symptoms that showed up in the alert
  • The change that restores service, made in a way the team can see
  • A runbook note or a permanent fix, so the same escalation does not return next week
  • A view on whether the design itself is what made the fault hard to see

This is an operations background, not a workshop exercise

Neil spent eight years as senior third-line support in enterprise telecommunications, operating Linux session border controllers for accounts including HMRC and Barclays, and writing more than 1,000 technical processes so repeat escalations dropped. He then ran incidents across a contact-centre platform and a hosted telephony estate, with deep-dive troubleshooting on AWS, Linux, Docker, and Kubernetes, and a Linux estate of more than 200 servers.

More recently that has meant EKS services and ECS on EC2, plus Azure PaaS, with CloudWatch, CloudTrail, Azure Monitor, the Elastic Stack, and OpenSearch as the places evidence actually lives.

Call this when a live fault needs a senior engineer, or when the same fault keeps coming back and the team is tired of restoring service without knowing why. If the answer is a platform change rather than another restart, the architecture and Terraform pages describe the follow-on work.

Describe the fault, the cloud, and how long it has been hurting. The contact page is the place to do that.
